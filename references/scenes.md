# Scene and beat techniques

Use these when writing the scene list in the outline and when drafting each scene.

## Scene and sequel

**Source:** Dwight Swain, *Techniques of the Selling Writer*, 1965

A scene has three parts: Goal, Conflict, Disaster. A sequel gives the character time to respond: Reaction, Dilemma, Decision. The best disasters are "yes, but" (you get it, with a cost) or "no, and" (you fail, and it gets worse).

**Use in games when:** you plan the rhythm between action and downtime. A fight or puzzle is a scene; a camp talk or a map choice is a sequel.

**Writer:**
- State the scene's goal in one line, then the obstacle, then the disaster.
- Write a "yes, but" or "no, and" outcome, not a clean win or flat loss.
- Use the sequel to turn the disaster into the next decision.

**Editor asks:**
- Does each scene have a clear goal, a conflict and an outcome that complicates things?
- Does each sequel end in a decision that starts the next scene?

## Value shift

**Source:** Robert McKee, *Story*, 1997

Every scene turns a value from positive to negative, or the reverse: safe to in danger, trusted to betrayed, hopeful to hopeless. If nothing turns, the scene is not doing work.

**Use in games when:** you are deciding what to cut. A scene with no shift gets cut or merged into a neighbour.

**Writer:**
- Write the value at the start and at the end of each scene in the outline.
- If both are the same, merge the scene or add a turn.
- Mark the line where the shift happens.

**Editor asks:**
- Is there a stated value at the start and a different one at the end?
- Can the editor point to the line where it turns?

## Enter late, leave early

**Source:** William Goldman

Start the scene just before the conflict and leave once the value has turned. Skip greetings, travel and wrap-up.

**Use in games when:** you are writing voiced or localized lines. Every voice-over line costs money, and every line costs translation. Cut what the player does not need.

**Writer:**
- Delete the first and last lines of the draft and check whether the scene still works.
- Start on the disagreement, not on the arrival.
- End on the turn or the decision.

**Editor asks:**
- Does the scene open at or just before the conflict?
- Does it end soon after the value turns?

## Try-fail cycles

**Source:** Brandon Sanderson; Dwight Swain

The protagonist fails two or three times, with rising stakes, before succeeding. Each failure teaches or costs something.

**Use in games when:** a boss, a quest or a puzzle story needs a long arc. The failures can be gameplay (a lost fight, a failed check) as well as scenes.

**Writer:**
- Plan two or three failed attempts with different approaches.
- Make each failure worse or more informative than the last.
- Let the final success use something learned from failing.

**Editor asks:**
- Are there at least two failed attempts before success?
- Does each attempt differ from the one before and raise the stakes?

## Stakes escalation

**Source:** General screenwriting practice

Stakes grow from personal, to community, to world, or become more intimate with each step. They must be clear in every act so the player knows what a loss means.

**Use in games when:** the story runs for hours. A flat stake makes the middle feel like a grind.

**Writer:**
- Write the stake for each act in one line.
- Raise it either in scale or in how close it gets to the main character.
- State what the player loses if they fail.

**Editor asks:**
- Is the stake stated for every act?
- Is each act's stake larger or more personal than the one before?
