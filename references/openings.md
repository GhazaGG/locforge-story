# Openings and reveals

How to start, what to withhold, and how to plant and pay off information.

## In medias res

**Source:** Horace, *Ars Poetica*

Start in the middle of the action and let backstory come later. The audience asks "what happened?" and "what next?" at the same time.

**Use in games when:** the player should be playing in the first minute. Teach controls inside the action, not in a tutorial before it.

**Writer:**
- Open on a moment of pressure the player can act on.
- Give only the context needed to understand the next move.
- Deliver backstory later, as a reward or a reveal.

**Editor asks:**
- Is the player acting or choosing within the first scene?
- Does the opening avoid explaining backstory before the first action?

## Cold open

**Source:** Television convention

A scene that plays before the title or menu and grabs attention. It may be a flash-forward to the climax.

**Use in games when:** you want a playable prologue before the title card. Give any flash-forward a clear frame (a caption, a visual change) so the player is not lost.

**Writer:**
- Keep the cold open short and end it on a question.
- Mark a flash-forward with a frame such as "Three days earlier".
- Make sure the main story actually reaches the moment shown.

**Editor asks:**
- Does the cold open end on a question or hook?
- If it is a flash-forward, is it clearly framed and later paid off?

## Hook in the first minutes

**Source:** Screenwriting practice

A dramatic question must appear within the first one to three minutes. The question can be a mystery, a threat, or a choice.

**Use in games when:** the player has not committed yet, especially in a demo or a first session. The editor counts lines before the first question or choice.

**Writer:**
- State the dramatic question in the first scene.
- Place the first choice or question early, ideally before the player has read a page.
- Cut setup lines that come before it.

**Editor asks:**
- Is there a question or choice within the first few lines, as counted by the editor?
- Can the editor state the dramatic question in one sentence?

## Iceberg / need-to-know exposition

**Source:** Ernest Hemingway

Show only a small part of what the author knows. Give the player information when they need it, not before.

**Use in games when:** the world has lore. The editor flags more than three new proper nouns in the first scene as a lore dump.

**Writer:**
- Introduce at most three new names in the opening scene.
- Hold world details until a choice or a puzzle depends on them.
- Keep the full lore in the bible and let the script show a fraction.

**Editor asks:**
- Does the first scene introduce three or fewer new proper nouns?
- Is each piece of exposition needed for the current decision?

## Mystery box

**Source:** J.J. Abrams

An open question pulls the player forward. The box works only if the author knows what is inside.

**Use in games when:** the story depends on discovery: investigation, exploration, or a hidden past. Every mystery needs an `answer` in the bible.

**Writer:**
- For each mystery, write the real answer in the bible before drafting.
- Plan at least two clues and one reveal for each mystery.
- Drip answers so each one opens a smaller question.

**Editor asks:**
- Does the bible give an `answer` for every mystery?
- Are the clues in the script enough to make the answer fair?

## Chekhov's gun / setup and payoff

**Source:** Anton Chekhov (attributed); common screenwriting practice

What is introduced gets used, and what is used was introduced. A setup with no payoff is clutter; a payoff with no setup is a cheat.

**Use in games when:** you introduce items, skills, characters or lines that matter later. Tag both ends in the script (`setup:<name>`, `payoff:<name>`) so the script can verify them.

**Writer:**
- Tag the setup and the payoff with the same name.
- Place the setup before the payoff in every route that can reach it.
- Remove the setup if you cut the payoff.

**Editor asks:**
- Does every `setup:` have a `payoff:` on a reachable path?
- Does every payoff rest on something the player saw earlier?

## Foreshadowing and red herrings

**Source:** Fiction craft

Foreshadowing is an honest clue to what will come. A red herring is a false clue that is still fair: with hindsight, it was explainable.

**Use in games when:** the story is a mystery or a detective story. Every clue is listed in the bible so the author can check that the fair-play rule holds.

**Writer:**
- List each real clue and each red herring in the bible.
- Give each red herring an explanation that holds in hindsight.
- Do not hide a clue only in an item the player may never open.

**Editor asks:**
- Are all clues in the script also in the bible?
- Does every red herring have an explanation consistent with the answer?

## Dramatic irony

**Source:** Alfred Hitchcock (the bomb under the table)

The audience knows more than the character does. Tension comes from watching someone walk toward the thing the audience knows about.

**Use in games when:** you can show the player something the hero does not know. For example, show the villain's scene before the hero learns of the plan.

**Writer:**
- Pick one fact the player will know and the character will not.
- Show it in a cutscene, a document, or an overheard line.
- Let the character act on the missing knowledge for at least one scene.

**Editor asks:**
- Does the player know the key fact before the character does?
- Does the character act in a way that the missing fact makes tense?
