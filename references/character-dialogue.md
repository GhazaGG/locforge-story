# Character and dialogue techniques

Use these for the bible's character sheets and for every line of dialogue.

## Want, need and the lie

**Source:** K.M. Weiland, *Creating Character Arcs*

The want is the outer goal. The need is the inner requirement. The lie is a false belief that keeps the character from the need. A positive arc moves from lie to truth; a flat arc has a character who already holds the truth and changes the world; a negative arc ends in a worse lie or in tragedy.

**Use in games when:** a main character or companion should change over the story. Decide the arc type per character in the bible.

**Writer:**
- Write want, need and lie in the bible, one line each.
- Pick the arc: positive, flat or negative.
- Plan at least one scene where the lie costs the character something.

**Editor asks:**
- Does each major character have want, need, lie and an arc type in the bible?
- Does the outline show the lie being tested or broken?

## Ghost or wound

**Source:** K.M. Weiland; John Truby

The ghost (Weiland) or wound (Truby) is the past event that made the character believe the lie. It need not be told in full, but the writer must know it.

**Use in games when:** a character's behaviour needs a reason, such as a companion who refuses to trust. Reveal the ghost slowly, ideally through a choice or a side scene.

**Writer:**
- Write the event, who was involved, and the belief it left behind.
- Place hints before the reveal.
- Reveal it at a moment when it explains a present choice.

**Editor asks:**
- Is the ghost in the bible?
- Does the character's current behaviour trace back to it?

## Voice profile

**Source:** Industry practice

A voice profile lists, per character: vocabulary, sentence length, verbal tics, register (formal or slang), and what they never say. These map to the bible's `voice` and `avoid` fields.

**Use in games when:** the cast shares a script and lines must sound like different people. The editor compares against the voice stats in `story_check`.

**Writer:**
- Fill in the `voice` and `avoid` fields before drafting a character's first line.
- Cover your name tags and check that you can still tell who speaks.
- Keep tics rare enough to stay fresh, about one per scene.

**Editor asks:**
- Can the speaker be identified from the line alone?
- Do the lines match the voice stats and the `avoid` list?

## Subtext

**Source:** Robert McKee; "on the nose" dialogue is the common error

Characters do not say exactly what they feel. They talk about something else, deflect, or say the opposite.

**Use in games when:** an emotional beat matters. Players read tone from short lines, so subtext is how those lines carry weight.

**Writer:**
- Write the true feeling in a note, then write a line that avoids stating it.
- Use an object, a task or a joke as the cover.
- Keep one plain statement for the scene's turn.

**Editor asks:**
- Does any line state the character's feeling outright without need?
- Could the same moment work with an action replacing the line?

## Show, don't tell

**Source:** Writing craft; often attributed to Anton Chekhov

Show the story through what happens, not through summary. In games, the strongest shows are player action, the environment and the interface.

**Use in games when:** you are about to write narration. Look first for a way to let the player do or see it.

**Writer:**
- Replace a narrated fact with an object, an animation or a choice.
- Use narration only for what cannot be shown.
- Keep inner thoughts short and specific.

**Editor asks:**
- Is any narration or exposition replaceable by player action or the environment?
- Does the scene give a concrete image instead of a label?

## Dialogue as conflict

**Source:** Dramatic writing practice

Each exchange is a negotiation. At least one speaker wants something from the other and meets resistance.

**Use in games when:** a conversation would otherwise be an info dump. Give the NPC a reason to hold back.

**Writer:**
- For each dialogue, write what each speaker wants.
- Let the player's choice press on or yield to that want.
- Avoid pairs of lines that only agree.

**Editor asks:**
- Does each speaker want something in this exchange?
- Is there resistance, even a mild one?

## Barks

**Source:** AAA game practice (for example, GDC talks on *The Last of Us*)

Barks are short lines that repeat in play: combat callouts, idle lines, pickup reactions. They need variants so they do not grate.

**Use in games when:** a line plays more than a few times. Write three to five variants of each, at most about eight words per bark.

**Writer:**
- Write 3 to 5 variants per trigger.
- Keep each under about 8 words.
- Differ in wording, not only in synonyms, and avoid names or numbers that need placeholders.

**Editor asks:**
- Does each bark have 3 to 5 variants?
- Is each variant about 8 words or fewer?

## Writing for voice-over and subtitles

**Source:** Subtitle guidelines in the BBC and Netflix style

Voice-over lines should be short and speakable, with a clear speaker. Subtitles run about 42 characters per line and at most 2 lines.

**Use in games when:** the game is voiced or ships subtitles. Check actor-facing readability and screen fit together.

**Writer:**
- Keep lines short and read them aloud.
- Always tag the speaker and add a direction note (tone, volume, pace) for the actor.
- Avoid homophones and tongue-twisters that confuse the ear.

**Editor asks:**
- Does each line fit about 42 characters by 2 lines for subtitles?
- Is the speaker clear, with a direction note where tone is not obvious?
