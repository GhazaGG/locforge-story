# Story structure frameworks

Pick one framework per story at intake and save it in the bible's `framework` setting. The editor checks the outline against that framework only. Frameworks are lenses, not templates: a beat may be skipped if the outline says why.

## Save the Cat!

**Source:** Blake Snyder, *Save the Cat!*, 2005; novel version by Jessica Brody, 2018

A 15-beat sheet. Each beat has a rough position as a percentage of the story, which lets you check pacing against the outline.

1. Opening Image (0-1%)
2. Theme Stated (5%)
3. Set-Up (1-10%)
4. Catalyst (10%)
5. Debate (10-20%)
6. Break into Two (20%)
7. B Story (22%)
8. Fun and Games (20-50%)
9. Midpoint (50%)
10. Bad Guys Close In (50-75%)
11. All Is Lost (75%)
12. Dark Night of the Soul (75-80%)
13. Break into Three (80%)
14. Finale (80-99%)
15. Final Image (99-100%)

**Use in games when:** the game is a linear narrative, a single-route visual novel, or cutscene-driven. Fun and Games is the core gameplay loop, not a run of story scenes.

**Writer:**
- Map each beat to a named scene or level in the outline, with its approximate percentage.
- Treat Fun and Games as the stretch where the player does the main verb; plan story beats around it, not through it.
- Make the Catalyst something the player does or sees, not a text wall.

**Editor asks:**
- Does every beat (or a stated skip) have a scene in the outline at roughly its position?
- Does Fun and Games match the game's core loop rather than just exposition?

## Three-act structure

**Source:** Syd Field, *Screenplay*, 1979

Act 1 is setup (about 25%) and ends at Plot Point 1, which sends the hero into a new situation. Act 2 is confrontation (about 50%), contains the midpoint, and ends at Plot Point 2, the lowest or most committed moment. Act 3 is resolution (about 25%).

1. Act 1: setup, ending at Plot Point 1 (~25%)
2. Act 2: confrontation, midpoint in the middle, ending at Plot Point 2 (~75%)
3. Act 3: resolution (~25%)

**Use in games when:** you need a plain default frame for a linear campaign.

**Writer:**
- Name Plot Point 1 and Plot Point 2 as concrete events before outlining anything else.
- Put a midpoint that changes what the player believes or can do.
- Keep Act 3 short; the player wants the payoff, not another hour.

**Editor asks:**
- Are Plot Point 1 and Plot Point 2 named events in the outline?
- Does the midpoint change the goal, the stakes or the player's knowledge?

## Hero's Journey

**Source:** Joseph Campbell, 1949; Christopher Vogler, *The Writer's Journey* (12 stages)

A twelve-stage cycle: the hero leaves the ordinary world, is tested, survives an ordeal, and returns changed.

1. Ordinary World
2. Call to Adventure
3. Refusal of the Call
4. Meeting the Mentor
5. Crossing the Threshold
6. Tests, Allies, Enemies
7. Approach to the Inmost Cave
8. Ordeal
9. Reward
10. The Road Back
11. Resurrection
12. Return with the Elixir

**Use in games when:** the game is an RPG or adventure with a clear departure and return. It is the most cliche-prone framework, so use it as a check, not a script.

**Writer:**
- Pick the stages that serve the story; do not force a mentor or a refusal if they add nothing.
- For each stage used, name what is different from the genre default.
- Make the Elixir something the player can feel in the world or the mechanics.

**Editor asks:**
- Is each stage used a specific scene, not a generic label?
- Does the outline avoid the stock mentor-dies, chosen-one defaults, or justify them?

## Story Circle

**Source:** Dan Harmon

Eight steps around a circle: a character in a comfort zone wants something, goes into the unfamiliar, pays a price, and returns changed.

1. You (comfort zone)
2. Need
3. Go
4. Search
5. Find
6. Take (pay the price)
7. Return
8. Change

**Use in games when:** the unit is a quest, chapter or episode that starts and ends at a hub. The return step maps to the player coming back to base.

**Writer:**
- Write the Need and the Take first; they define the quest.
- Make the price in step 6 cost something the player cares about (an item, a relationship, a place).
- Show the Change at the hub: a different line, a different NPC reaction.

**Editor asks:**
- Does step 6 impose a real cost?
- Does step 8 show up in changed hub content, not only in narration?

## Kishotenketsu

**Source:** East Asian four-part structure (Chinese, Korean, Japanese tradition)

A four-part form that needs no central conflict. Ki introduces, Sho develops, Ten brings a twist or a new angle, and Ketsu reconciles the parts.

1. Ki: introduction
2. Sho: development
3. Ten: twist or new angle
4. Ketsu: reconciliation

**Use in games when:** the game is cozy, slice of life, or in the style of Japanese visual novels. Surprise comes from a new viewpoint, not from a fight.

**Writer:**
- Do not add a villain or a threat to fill a gap; let Ten be a reframing, a new character, or a hidden connection.
- Spend most of the length in Ki and Sho, building a place and its people.
- Let Ketsu connect Ten back to Ki without a confrontation.

**Editor asks:**
- Is Ten a genuine new angle rather than a conflict bolted on?
- Does Ketsu link back to what Ki introduced?

## Seven-point structure

**Source:** Dan Wells

Seven plot points, planned backwards from the Resolution so every earlier point leads toward it.

1. Hook
2. Plot Turn 1
3. Pinch 1
4. Midpoint
5. Pinch 2
6. Plot Turn 2
7. Resolution

**Use in games when:** the game has multiple endings. Plan one Resolution per ending and work backwards from each; the Hook and early points can be shared.

**Writer:**
- Write the Resolution first; then set the Hook as its opposite.
- Make each Pinch apply pressure that forces the player to act, not only to watch.
- For multiple endings, mark which points are shared and where routes split.

**Editor asks:**
- Does each ending have its own Resolution, with the earlier points leading to it?
- Do the pinches push the player into action?

## MICE quotient

**Source:** Orson Scott Card; expanded by Mary Robinette Kowal

Every story thread is one of four types: Milieu (explore a place), Inquiry (answer a question), Character (change a person), Event (restore order after disruption). Threads close in reverse order of opening, like nested brackets.

**Use in games when:** you are tracking quests and subplots. Pair it with `setup:` and `payoff:` tags so the script can check that opened threads are closed.

**Writer:**
- Label each quest or subplot with its MICE type in the outline.
- List thread open and close in order; the last opened is the first closed.
- Tag the opening line `setup:<name>` and the closing line `payoff:<name>`.

**Editor asks:**
- Does every thread opened have a close, and in reverse order?
- Is each thread's type clear from the outline?

## Promise, progress, payoff

**Source:** Brandon Sanderson, BYU lectures

The opening promises a kind of story. The middle shows progress toward that promise. The ending pays it off in the way the opening suggested.

**Use in games when:** the prologue or tutorial sets expectations. If it promises a mystery, the game must deliver a mystery, not a combat game with a thin plot.

**Writer:**
- Write the promise in one sentence and check it against the first scene.
- At each act boundary, show visible progress: a clue, a relationship, a power gained.
- Write the ending to answer the promise, not just to stop.

**Editor asks:**
- Does the first scene or tutorial promise the genre the ending delivers?
- Is there visible progress at each act boundary?

## Fichtean curve

**Source:** John Gardner, *The Art of Fiction*

Start in crisis. Then run a chain of rising crises with little setup, and finish with a short resolution.

**Use in games when:** the game is action, horror, short, or made for a game jam, where setup time is a luxury.

**Writer:**
- Open on the first crisis and give background only inside later crises.
- List the crises in order and make each one worse than the last.
- Keep the resolution to one scene.

**Editor asks:**
- Does each crisis escalate beyond the previous one?
- Is the resolution short and tied to the final crisis?
